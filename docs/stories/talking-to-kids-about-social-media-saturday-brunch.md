# Have You Talked to Your Kids About Social Media? — A Saturday Brunch

*A story for the grownups in the room.*
*Book Thirteen of the Scipraxian Tales.*

---

## A note before the story

This is the dad's Tale. Specifically the dad-with-half-time-custody — Friday evening to Sunday evening every other weekend, thirty hours that have to do the work the rest of the month doesn't get to do.

It is built around five of the Twelve Variables: **Inquiry, Humility, Time, Fear, and Inclusion.** And it puts a thirteen-year-old in the booth across from her father at her favorite cafe and asks him to be the dad he wants to be in the time he actually has.

The asking is the tap on the shoulder. The story is what happens after you turn around.

---

## Chapter 1 — Friday Night

Have you talked to your kids about social media?

Not the dad-jokes-on-the-group-text version. Not the ex-wife-told-me version. The real thing — the call from your ex on a Friday night at nine-eleven PM giving you a heads-up that your daughter has had a hard week and you have her tomorrow morning at nine and you have not been good at this part yet.

Ricardo was on the couch in his one-bedroom apartment downtown when the phone rang. The TV was on but he wasn't watching. He had been thinking about whether to make pancakes tomorrow morning or go to *La Esquina* for brunch. La Esquina was Sofia's favorite. Sofia was thirteen.

"Carmen."

"Hey. You have her tomorrow."

"I have her tomorrow."

"I wanted to give you a heads-up. She's had a hard week. There was a thing on Snap. I don't have all of it. She's been in her room more than usual. She's been quieter at the table. I'm not asking you to fix it. I'm telling you so you're not surprised when she's quieter than normal tomorrow."

Ricardo sat up.

"OK. Carmen — thank you. Do you want me to do anything specific or just be aware."

"Just be aware. I think she might want to tell you. She might not. Don't push her. If she wants to tell you, listen. If she doesn't, just be her dad."

"OK."

"Ricardo."

"Yeah."

"…I'm going to be honest with you. I don't fully understand what happened on Snap. I asked her. She gave me the eight-word version and went to her room. I don't know what to do with the eight-word version. So if she tells you the longer version, will you tell me. Tomorrow night when you drop her off. We can talk on the porch."

"…Yeah Carmen. We can talk on the porch."

"OK. Goodnight Ricardo."

"Goodnight Carmen."

He hung up.

He sat on the couch for a minute. He turned the TV off. The apartment was quiet in the way the apartment had been quiet for four years — too quiet on the off-weekends, almost-too-loud-with-Sofia on the on-weekends, and the math of those two never actually added up to a normal week's worth of being a dad.

He went to the kitchen. He opened the fridge. He looked at the eggs and the bacon and the carton of orange juice and the chocolate bar he had bought Sofia for hot chocolate and the half-bottle of wine from last week.

He closed the fridge.

He thought: *I have thirty hours.*

He thought: *Carmen called me. That is new. Carmen calling me is the first new thing in a year.*

He thought: *I do not know what Snap is. I have a Snap account I have not opened in two years. I have not been good at this part. I am going to be good at this part.*

He picked up his own phone. He opened Snap. He had not opened it in two years. The notifications had piled up — fifty-eight of them. He cleared them. He looked at the interface. He did not understand half of it.

He closed Snap. He put the phone down.

He went to bed at eleven-twenty. He did not sleep well.

## Chapter 2 — La Esquina

Saturday morning. Ricardo picked Sofia up from Carmen's at nine-oh-four. He was four minutes late. Sofia was on the front step with her duffel and her phone. She was in her Saturday-with-Dad clothes — jeans, the gray hoodie she always brought, the bracelet her mom had given her at twelve that she had not taken off in fourteen months.

"Hi mija."

"Hi Dad."

She got in the front seat. She did not put her phone in her bag. She kept it in her hand. He noticed. He did not comment.

"La Esquina?"

"…yeah."

He drove. The radio was the soft-rock station that had been the radio station since Sofia was six. They did not talk on the drive. Sofia looked out the window. Ricardo did not push.

La Esquina was the cafe on Mariposa Street with the green awning and the booth in the back that Sofia had picked the first time they had come here, three years ago, two months after the divorce. The booth was empty when they got there. Sofia slid in. Ricardo slid in across from her.

"You want the pancakes or the eggs."

"…eggs. With bacon. And the hash browns."

"Hot chocolate?"

"Yeah. Two marshmallows."

He went to the counter. He ordered. He came back with two waters. He sat down.

He did not say *your mom called me last night.* He did not say *what happened on Snap.* He did not say *are you OK.*

He said: "Mija. I have a question for you and I want you to know I am asking because I am paying attention, not because I'm being a cop. Is that OK?"

Sofia looked at him. She was thirteen. She knew what *I am paying attention not being a cop* meant in dad-language. She had heard him use it before, at twelve, when she had been crying about something her mom had said and he had not pushed. She nodded once.

"OK. Mom called me last night. She said you've had a hard week. She said something happened on Snap. She didn't push you on it. She gave me the heads-up so I wouldn't be surprised. I'm not pushing you either. If you want to tell me about it, I will listen. If you don't, we can just have brunch and I will not bring it up again. You pick. I will follow."

Sofia did not say anything for a minute. She picked up her water. She drank some.

She said: "Dad. I want to tell you. But I'm scared you're going to be mad at me."

Ricardo did the thing he had taught himself, after the divorce, to do in moments when his dad-brain wanted to react fast. He took a breath. He looked at her. He said the slow version of what he meant.

"Sofia. I am not going to be mad at you. I am pre-deciding right now, before you tell me what happened, that I am not going to be mad at you. Whatever you tell me, the not-mad is locked in. OK?"

"…OK."

"Take your time. The eggs are still coming."

---

## Chapter 3 — What Happened on Snap

Sofia took her time.

"OK. So. There's a girl in my grade named Hadley. We have been friends since fourth grade but we have been not really friends since this year. Hadley and me had a fight on Snap on Tuesday night about — about a thing that doesn't matter. I said something about another girl, Olivia, that I shouldn't have said. I said it in the chat with Hadley. I thought it was just me and Hadley.

"Hadley screenshotted what I said. Hadley posted the screenshot on her story Wednesday morning before school. By second period the whole grade had seen it. Olivia saw it. Olivia cried in the hallway. Olivia is a nice person and I said a not-nice thing about her in a chat I thought was private, and now everybody knows I said it, and Olivia is the one who got hurt, not me, even though I am also hurt, and I don't know how to fix it because I can't take back what I said and I can't make Hadley take down the screenshot."

Ricardo did not say anything for a minute. He was glad the eggs hadn't come yet because if they had come he would not have been able to eat them.

He said: "Sofia. Thank you for telling me. That is hard to say out loud and you said it cleanly. OK. Let me ask you a couple questions. Slowly. Take your time."

"OK."

"First question. The thing you said about Olivia. Was it true."

Sofia thought about it.

"…Some of it was. Some of it was me being mean. I was mad at Hadley about something else and I said the meanest thing I could about Olivia because Olivia and Hadley are friends. I knew it would hurt Hadley if I said something mean about Olivia. I did it on purpose. But what I said — some of it was just mean. Not true. Just mean."

"OK. Second question. Have you talked to Olivia since."

"No. I haven't been able to look at her at school."

"Third question. If you could do one thing in the next forty-eight hours that would make this even one percent better, what would it be."

Sofia thought about this one for the longest. The eggs came. The waitress put them down. Ricardo nodded thanks. Sofia did not pick up her fork.

She said: "I would tell Olivia I am sorry. To her face. Not on Snap. In person. I would say it in the way that meant it. Then I would tell her she could be mad at me for as long as she needed. Then I would not bother her at school until she was ready to talk to me. And I would tell Hadley I am done being friends because Hadley is the one who put the screenshot up and Hadley is the one who knew what would happen when she did and Hadley is not somebody I want to be in a chat with anymore."

"Sofia. That is a really good answer. That is what a thirteen-year-old who is sorry would do."

"…I am sorry, Dad."

"I know mija."

"And I am scared."

"Of what."

"Of how hard it is going to be on Monday."

"Yeah. Monday is going to be hard. I will tell you the truest thing I know about Monday. The first hour is the hardest hour. After the first hour, it gets a little easier. After the first day, it gets a little easier than that. The thing you said on Snap is going to be in the air for a while. The thing you do on Monday — apologizing to Olivia in the way you just said you would — is the thing that starts the air clearing. Not all at once. But it starts."

"OK Dad."

"Eat your eggs. They are getting cold."

She ate the eggs. She ate the bacon. She drank the hot chocolate. She did not cry at the table. She cried a little when they were walking back to the car.

## Chapter 4 — The Walk to the Car

They walked the four blocks back to the car. The morning had warmed up. Ricardo had his hand on her shoulder. Sofia was crying the small kind of crying — not loud, not with sounds, just the wet kind that drips down the face on its own when you don't have the energy to hold it.

He did not say *don't cry.* He did not say *it's going to be OK.* He said: "I want to ask you for one thing, mija."

"…OK."

"I want you to teach me Snap. This weekend. Not because I am going to look over your shoulder forever. Because I had a Snap account two years ago and I have not opened it. I do not know how the screenshot thing works. I do not know how the story thing works. I do not know what a *streak* is even though I have heard the word twenty times. I want you to teach me. So that the next time something happens — and there will be a next time, mija, that's just how it works — I am not the dad who has to ask his ex-wife to translate. I want to be the dad who you can call."

Sofia looked at him.

"Dad. You'd let me teach you?"

"Mija. I am asking you to teach me. There is a difference."

"…OK."

"And here is the other part. I am going to tell your mom what you told me at brunch. Tonight, on her porch, when I drop you off. She is going to listen the same way I listened. She and I are going to be on the same page about Monday. We are not going to fix it for you because you have already said how you want to fix it. We are going to be the two adults who know what your plan is and we are going to be ready when Monday lands. You good with that?"

"Yeah Dad."

"And if you change the plan between now and Monday, you tell me or you tell Mom. Either of us. We are not going to be mad. The plan can change. The not-mad is locked in. OK?"

"OK."

He squeezed her shoulder. She leaned into him for half a step. They got to the car.

---

## Chapter 5 — Sunday Evening, Carmen's Porch

Sunday evening. Five-fifty-three PM. Ricardo dropped Sofia off at Carmen's house. Sofia took her duffel inside. Carmen came out onto the porch. Ricardo stood at the bottom of the steps.

"Carmen."

"Ricardo. Come up."

He came up. They sat on the porch chairs. They had not sat on the porch chairs together since the divorce. The chairs were the same chairs they had picked out at the IKEA in 2018 when they had still been *they.*

"She told me," Ricardo said.

"Yeah?"

"At brunch yesterday. She told me what happened with Hadley and Olivia. She told me her plan for Monday. She is going to apologize to Olivia in person, then give Olivia space. She is going to end the friendship with Hadley."

"…OK. That's a good plan. That's a thirteen-year-old plan."

"It is. She came up with it. I just listened."

"Ricardo. Thank you for listening. I — I called you on Friday because I didn't know what to do with the eight-word version she gave me. I didn't expect — thank you."

"Carmen. I asked her to teach me Snap this weekend. I wanted to tell you that. I had the app I hadn't opened. I asked her to walk me through it. We did it Saturday afternoon. We did it again this morning. I am going to be on it now — not to surveille her, because we agreed on what *on it* means, but so I am not the dad asking my ex-wife to translate the next time something happens."

Carmen was quiet for a second.

"…Ricardo. I'm going to ask her to teach me too. I haven't been on it either."

"I would do it together if you want. Tomorrow night on the phone. The three of us. We make her the teacher. She walks us both through it. We take notes."

"…Yeah. Let's do that."

"And Carmen. About Monday."

"Yeah."

"Sofia is scared. The first hour is the hardest hour. I told her that. If you can — pick her up at school on Monday. Just for that day. Don't let her ride the bus home."

"OK."

"Thank you."

"Ricardo. Thank *you.* For Saturday. For listening to her. For asking her to teach you."

He stood up. She stood up. They did not hug — they had not hugged in four years — but they shook hands the way they had at the lawyer's office the day the divorce had been finalized, and the handshake meant something different this time.

He drove home. The apartment was quiet. He opened Snap. The notifications were already three deep — Sofia had sent him a *streak* message because they had agreed yesterday to start a streak. He sent one back.

Then he typed her a longer message: *Mija. Mom and I are going to learn Snap together tomorrow night on the phone. You are going to teach us both. Bring your patience. We will be slow learners. I love you. Goodnight.*

She replied: *ok dad. love you too. goodnight.*

The asking had become the asking-back. The ripple had begun, on a Sunday evening at six-twelve PM in two apartments on opposite sides of a city, between a divorced father and a divorced mother and a thirteen-year-old daughter with a hard week ahead, who would learn together — badly and then slightly less badly — a tool none of them had been good at yet.

Monday came. Sofia apologized to Olivia in the hallway before second period. The first hour was the hardest hour. The first day was the hardest day. The week got a little easier. The streak with her dad reached fifteen days. Carmen learned what a streak was. Ricardo learned what a story was. The three of them got better, badly and then slightly less badly, at the thing none of them had wanted to need to be good at.

## A note after the story

The Variables in this Tale were named by Ricardo's *I am pre-deciding right now, before you tell me what happened, that I am not going to be mad at you,* by Sofia's eight-word answer to *if you could do one thing,* and by the handshake on Carmen's porch on Sunday at six-oh-eight PM. They were:

- **Inquiry** — *I am paying attention, not being a cop.* The frame Ricardo set before he asked the first question. Inquiry that opens a door instead of breaking one is the move a thirteen-year-old can step through without losing face.
- **Humility** — *I had a Snap account I had not opened in two years. I do not know what a streak is. I want you to teach me.* Said by a thirty-nine-year-old project manager to his thirteen-year-old daughter at the car door. Variable 2 in plain clothes.
- **Time** — Ricardo gets thirty hours. The Tale's stakes live inside that constraint. Spending the thirty hours on listening — *the eggs are still coming* — is the Variable made manifest. Time is the only one that doesn't grow back. He spent it on her. That counted.
- **Fear** — Sofia's *I am scared* about Monday. Ricardo's quieter fear of being a half-time dad who got it wrong. Both fears were real. Both fears were named. Naming made them workable instead of paralyzing.
- **Inclusion** — *I am asking you to teach me. There is a difference.* And the three-of-us-on-the-phone-tomorrow-night with Sofia as the teacher and Ricardo and Carmen as the students. The kid in the teaching seat is Inclusion at the most direct scale. The Tale takes it literally on Monday night at seven-forty PM when the call happens.

Snap entered Sofia's seventh-grade life *on* her — through Hadley's screenshot button, on the platform's terms, with the screenshot persistent in the air long after the chat was over. The conversation Ricardo had with her at La Esquina entered her life *with* her, because he asked her to be the teller and she chose the order. The conversation Carmen and Ricardo had on the porch entered the *family's* life *with* the family — co-parenting that had been four years post-divorce and had finally found a thing the two of them could do together for the kid both of them loved, in the same room, on the same page, at the same time. Same kid. Same week. Multiple consent geometries in one weekend. The shape is choosable, even thirty hours at a time.
